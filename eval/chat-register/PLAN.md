## What declaring the act buys (declarations.mjs, EXPLORATORY: 600 hash-picked messages per source, the act layer's pure functions on the reader's universal marks only)

The declaration is a measurable thing in itself, which a router's passing decision is not.
- **Profile of what it declares** (working act): SMS social 38%, statement 37%, question 14%, request 6%, unread 5%; CoSEM social 39%, unread 24%, statement 21%; IRC statement 39%, question 27%, social 23%, unread 8%; the formal control statement 59%, question 21%, unread 10%. It admits two acts hold (open) on 3-6% of real messages and a mark opposes a claim on 10-24%.
- **It survives perturbations that break the reading under it.** The working act is unchanged in 96% of messages when everything is lowercased (the reader alone read those identically 17% of the time), 98% under one letter swap (reader 90%); but only 68% when the final punctuation is dropped (reader 83%): the declaration leans on that mark.
- **Its fragility is predictable from the marks it names.** Pooled over real chat and the three perturbations, a working act supported by one mark survives 91%, by two marks 97%, by three or more 100% (6 cases). Under a missing final mark: 71% for one mark against 98% for two. When the one mark is the final "?", the act survives only 61% (all other single marks 95-98%). So "a question resting on a question mark alone" is the claim to keep open; a wh-word or an auxiliary before the subject makes it robust. Stability is not correctness: there is no gold here, and a stable wrong declaration also survives.

## The Brydon test applied to what the measurements show (names a valve as a valve)

Per `native/docs/THE-MAN-IN-THE-BOX.md`: what would the man in the box do here? Three mechanisms fail it, with the evidence:
1. **`no_predicate` makes a message social, and "Okay." answers every social remark.** Of the 618 messages the act layer declares social from marks alone (600 per source, three sources), only 63 (10%) are interjection-only by the reader's own marks. The rest, 555, are names ("Aishwarya"), a bot's output ("STATUS 9RA3ZAQC"), commands ("!pt", "!skype > bob3213243"), answers addressed to someone ("lulu: nope.. not really"), requests ("one sec", "Quickly coooome"), questions ("Which company?"), and laughter and thanks (which are social). A man says "Okay." to an acknowledgement, not to a name or a command; for those he would say he doesn't follow. A valve (a special case added so every fragment gets an answer): social needs positive evidence, and a bare fragment with nothing pending is open (a topic, an answer, a label, a remark), not social.
2. **"A bare name is a topic" looks it up outside.** 29 one-word messages (7.4% of 390) sent the word to Wikipedia. A man would not phone the library with a word he took for a greeting or noise. The limiter that was added ("only names, numbers or known words may leave") is defeated by its own "name" clause. What the man has to decide a word is a name is evidence (it is in the notebook as a person, it was introduced as one, a reference gives it as one), not capitalisation.
3. **The unknown-words guard itself** (7dfcdbf): a limiter added because one case went wrong (the Tamil question). It holds back lowercase names and stands in for the reader's missing no-codec gap; it should go when mechanic 5 lands. The document already says it is crude.
Not a valve, by the same test: the claims, the revision by "no", the notebook-first order, unread as an answer, the local dictionary for a word's meaning.

## What does the chat need a table of given names for? (pronoun-probe.mjs, 2026-11-23; EXPLORATORY, developer-written probes)

One thing, and the pairing check is not it (the chat can pair provisionally and say so; a claim is revisable). The one thing: with two people in a sentence and then "he" or "she", which person does the pronoun mean when the thread has said nothing else about either name? With no table loaded the chat answers 3 of 8 right: it binds to the first-named person whatever the pronoun's gender (3 of 3 when that is right, 0 of 5 when the pronoun means the second-named person: "Pierre met Natasha. She smiled." gives Pierre; "Tom met Dana. She smiled." gives Tom). The thread's own evidence overrides a table as soon as it exists, so a table only decides the first mention. And "Who smiled?" is answered "She." in 8 of 8: the pronoun itself, not the person.
Option (D), no giver: **declare the ambiguity and ask.** Two candidates, a gendered pronoun, no evidence about either name's gender: that is an open claim, and the chat says it ("Do you mean Pierre or Natasha?"), which is the man in the box's behaviour; the ask-back and the pairing exist (mechanic 7). A table would only save the question in the common cases, and carries the provenance and contamination problems above.

**Why this was weird, and where it stands** (the owner: "we should be tracking referents, why is this weird?"). The cast does track referents: Pierre is r1, Natasha is r2, "She" is a mention bound to a referent. What it tracked was the PICK, not the hypotheses: with no evidence either way the cast falls back to position (the subject touched last), records the pick as an *assumption on that referent* ("assumed f": Pierre is now taken to be a woman for later pronouns), counts the doubt (`pronounsContested`, here 1) and throws away which mention it was and who the alternative was; the chat then said "That would be Pierre." as a fact. And "Who smiled?" returned the mention's own text ("She.") because the answer path reads the role filler, not its referent. In the chat session's working tree at the time of writing (uncommitted edits to `engine.mjs` and `thread.mjs`; the cast, a reading file, is unchanged) the chat now asks ("Do you mean Pierre or Natasha?") and answers "Who smiled?" with "“She”, but I can't tell whether that is ...". Same eight probes: **0 right, 8 asked which, 0 wrong** (was 3 right, 0 asked, 5 wrong at 80c06d0). The price of no table is visible: it also asks "Do you mean Mary or John?" for "Mary called John. He answered.", which a table of given names would settle. The next step in the cast (a reading file, so the parsing owner's): keep the candidates and their evidence on the mention (`contestedMentions: { "1.1": { picked, alternatives } }`) instead of a counter and an assumption written onto the referent; additive, no number should change.

## How a proper noun should be gendered (the owner's three requirements: priors that weight it, a lookup of the name, titles give it away), tested on held-out novels; EXPLORATORY

`name-gender-giver.mjs` (cast cached per book; first 4,000 sentences of Pride and Prejudice, Little Women, Great Expectations, none of them among the four novels the repo's learned table was built from). Gold is the text's own pronouns (a named being with 8+ bound pronouns and 90% one side: 60 beings, 37 she and 23 he; 20 of them also decided by the cast's sign test); the cast was given no table of given names, so it cannot see what is tested. The gold is noisy (a fallback binding can put "he" on "Kate").
- **Title** (a title in any surface form of the being, read from the dictionary's gloss of the title, one cross-reference hop: "Abbreviation of Mister" then Mister): says something for 14 of 60 (23%), right 13 (93%). The one wrong is "Joe" in Great Expectations: the cast merged "Mrs. Joe" (his wife) into him; the title is evidence about a form, and a wrong merge carries it to the wrong being.
- **The name looked up** (the dictionary's glosses of the first name, "A female given name from Russian", read through the reader): says something for 31 of 60 (52%), right 29 (94%), one "both". The silent ones are mostly surnames (Darcy, Bingley, Wickham, Collins), a nickname (Marmee) and names the dictionary lacks or does not tag.
- **The learned table already in the repo** (counts from four other novels): says something for 4 of 60 (7%), right 4: right when it speaks, almost never speaking.
- **Title first, then the name's dictionary sense:** says something for 38 of 60 (63%), right 36 (95%).
The process this suggests, in order of strength, each step a revisable claim that records its source: (0) is it a person at all (`name-prior`: person against not-person), only persons are gendered; (1) what the text says about this referent: an explicit statement ("Natasha is a woman"), a title in any of its forms, a pro-form the clause attaches to it (the sign test needs several; one is enough for "Pierre took his hat"), a role noun in apposition; (2) the name looked up in a giver with provenance (the dictionary, already in the repo, licence CC BY-SA); (3) learned counts from many books, leaving out the book being read; (4) the weights of (1)-(3) are their measured agreement with held-out text (above), re-measured when a source changes, not typed; (5) when the sources disagree, or none speaks and a pronoun needs it, the claim stays open and the chat asks; (6) the text's own recurring pronouns overturn any prior, and every decision is a candidate with its sources shown. The referent boundary comes first: a title belongs to a form, and an identity edge that is not a merge must not carry it.
